# RR — What destroyed Napoleon's Grande Armée in 1812, and what would save the most soldiers?

Question: What actually destroyed Napoleon's Grande Armée in the 1812 Russian campaign — cold, typhus carried by lice, trench fever, starvation, desertion, or the legendary tin buttons crumbling in the frost? Weigh the modern evidence (including the 2001 Vilnius mass-grave DNA studies) against the popular myths, and say what a time-traveller would need to bring back to 1812 to save the most soldiers.

## Answer

Most of the army died of causes other than combat, and much of that dying began in summer. Of 300,000-350,000 dead, [100,000 were killed in combat and 200,000 died of other causes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_invasion_of_Russia), and one popular history page says Napoleon had lost [80,000 soldiers to typhus and dysentery one month into the campaign](https://www.montana.edu/historybug/napoleon/typhus-russia.html), well before the frost. The Vilnius grave DNA backs lice-borne and other infectious disease: [29% of 35 soldiers tested positive for trench fever or typhus DNA](https://www.infectioncontroltoday.com/view/new-study-identifies-louse-borne-diseases-ravaged-napoleons-army), and [a 2025 study found paratyphoid and louse-borne relapsing fever](https://www.pasteur.fr/en/press-area/press-documents/study-suggests-two-unsuspected-pathogens-struck-napoleon-s-army-during-retreat-russia-1812). The tin-button story is [contradicted by legible tin-alloy buttons from that same grave](https://www.straightdope.com/21343875/where-did-we-get-the-idea-crazy-people-think-they-re-napoleon-plus-did-tin-disease-contribute-to-nap). A time-traveller's best cargo would be delousing (insecticide, soap, hot-wash capacity) and clean-water or typhoid measures. That is an inference from the pathogens found; no source models it.

## Map

### Vilnius bioarchaeology
- Raoult et al. 2006 (J Infect Dis, [DOI 10.1086/498534](https://doi.org/10.1086/498534)), as reported by [Infection Control Today](https://www.infectioncontroltoday.com/view/new-study-identifies-louse-borne-diseases-ravaged-napoleons-army): "Three of the lice carried DNA from Bartonella quintana" (out of 5 lice recovered); "Dental pulp from seven soldiers contained DNA from B. quintana"; "pulp from three soldiers contained DNA from Rickettsia prowazakii, which causes epidemic typhus"; "29 percent of the soldiers tested had evidence of either ... infection." The number tested is 35, per the digger's report; this check did not quote it.
- Pasteur Institute 2025 aDNA study ([press release](https://www.pasteur.fr/en/press-area/press-documents/study-suggests-two-unsuspected-pathogens-struck-napoleon-s-army-during-retreat-russia-1812)): "the teeth of four tested positive for S. enterica Paratyphi C ... and two for B. recurrentis" (of 13 soldiers). It says "it is impossible to determine the extent to which these pathogens contributed to the extremely high mortality observed."
- Representativeness: [Archaeology Magazine](https://archive.archaeology.org/0209/abstracts/napoleon.html) reports that most of the dead were 15-20 years old, the excavation was rushed, and other mass graves lie nearby. (unquoted)

### Loss timeline
- Napoleon's force on entering Russia "exceeded 450,000 men" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_invasion_of_Russia)).
- "Just one month into the campaign, Napoleon had lost 80,000 soldiers to typhus and dysentery"; fevers and pink rashes began "within a few days of crossing the Niemen" ([Montana State](https://www.montana.edu/historybug/napoleon/typhus-russia.html)). This is a single popular source.
- Survivor counts disagree: Wikipedia's Grand Army article gives both 93,000 and 120,000 (unquoted).

### Cold, starvation and desertion
- The infobox gives "300,000–350,000 dead," with "100,000 killed in combat," "200,000 died from other causes," "50,000 died in captivity" and "50,000 deserted" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_invasion_of_Russia)).
- Retreat temperatures were about -31 at Vilna and -22 the day after the Berezina ([HistoryNet](https://historynet.com/1812-bitter-end/), unquoted). About 30,000 died in the Vilnius hospitals of typhus, wounds, frostbite and hunger ([War History Online](https://www.warhistoryonline.com/napoleon/real-reason-napoleons-invasion-russia-failed.html), unquoted).
- No source separates deaths from cold and starvation from deaths from disease.

### Tin-button myth
- "Tests performed with tin ingots suggest it'd take maybe 18 months at lowered temperatures to result in appreciable flaking." The Vilnius grave yielded "numerous regimental buttons, many made of tin alloy and still legible after 190 years in the ground" ([Straight Dope](https://www.straightdope.com/21343875/where-did-we-get-the-idea-crazy-people-think-they-re-napoleon-plus-did-tin-disease-contribute-to-nap)).
- Le Couteur, co-author of *Napoleon's Buttons*: "we don't really know if Napoleon's army actually had buttons made of tin" ([Design News](https://www.designnews.com/industry/did-tin-pest-cause-napoleon-s-wardrobe-failure-), unquoted).

### Counterfactual kit (inference)
- Louse-borne pathogens (typhus, trench fever, relapsing fever) point to delousing: bathing and hot-washing clothing at 55 °C or more ([phthiraptera.info](https://phthiraptera.myspecies.info/node/96229), unquoted). The 1944 Naples DDT campaign checked a typhus epidemic, though historians dispute how much of the decline DDT caused ([buttondown](https://buttondown.com/patricknovak1/archive/after-allied-forces-dusted-civilians-in-naples/), unquoted).
- Paratyphoid and dysentery point to boiled or clean water and sanitation (inference). Horse losses point to ice-calked shoes (snippet only; see open rabbit holes).
